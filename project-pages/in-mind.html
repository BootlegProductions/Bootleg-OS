Advanced Visual Design · University

In Mind

A concept for a calm, accessible wellbeing application combining mental-health resources with practical tracking tools.

Project brief

A university app concept exploring a more personal way to track mental and physical wellbeing through connected screens.

Design goal

The interface was intended to feel welcoming and low-stress: a streamlined onboarding experience, soft visual language and clear routes into the application's core services.

Skills

UI / UX Mobile design Pitch development Prototyping

Designing for everyday wellbeing

In Mind began as my Advanced Visual Design project: a concept for a mobile app that could make mental and physical wellbeing easier to track without making the experience feel clinical or overwhelming. I wanted one place for mood check-ins, sleep and medication reminders, and simple routes to exercises, meditation and support resources.

My own experience with epilepsy helped shape the brief. A health tool is more useful when it can fit around someone's actual routine and specific needs, rather than assuming every user wants the same set of features. The proposal explored medication schedules, recording health events and noticing patterns over time. These were design ideas for a concept, not claims that the prototype could diagnose a condition or detect an emergency.

From sketches to screens

I started with a brainstorm and mood board built around soft colour gradients, approachable illustrations and a calm first impression. The first screen set included a login page, a profile overview and a mental-health page where someone could record how they were feeling. I then made a short animated mock-up to demonstrate how the screens might work together in a digital advert.

Feedback on that first version was specific: some sections needed more space, labels could be friendlier, text overlapped, and the graph and icons needed clearer visual treatment. I revised the layout, simplified the logo, changed the login colours and reorganised the home screen so the most useful areas would be easier to find.

The mental-health tracker

On the mental-health screen, users could tap one of the little illustrated emoji faces to log their emotional state. The idea was to make a daily check-in feel simple and approachable: choose how you feel, save the entry, then see those entries build into a picture of how your mood has changed over time.

The colourful chart underneath was intended to make patterns easier to notice, while still leaving room for the person behind the data. The expressive faces were part of the app’s welcoming tone; in a developed version I would also want clear text labels so nobody has to interpret a colour or expression alone.

A more personal toolkit

The proposal grew beyond a single generic wellbeing dashboard. A user could choose relevant tools, such as a medication calendar, emotional tracker or connected health information, and leave out sections they did not need. I also explored the possibility of sharing information with trusted contacts or clinicians, though those ideas stayed at the concept stage and would require careful privacy and reliability work before becoming real features.

The finished university work was a set of designs and prototypes, supported by the pitch, sketchbook and feedback process. It taught me how much a useful health interface depends on clear language, considered priorities and iteration—not just a calming palette.
