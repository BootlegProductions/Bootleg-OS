How to start a YouTube channel with the equipment you already have

A direct first-upload walkthrough, followed by my story of returning to YouTube, learning to edit and gradually finding an audience.

By Oliver Lewis · Updated 20 October 2026 · About 5 min read

contents.exe / In this article
    The short version

    Pick one kind of video, make a small first episode with the equipment you already have, edit it, upload it clearly and make another. You can improve the setup as you learn.

    1. Choose something you can actually keep making

    Start with a subject you enjoy enough to return to. A gaming series, a hobby, a local project or a short editing experiment is easier to sustain when making it is part of the reward.

    Give the first video a small job. Show one build, explain one idea or record one useful part of a game. You do not need a complete brand strategy before learning how to finish an upload.

    2. Use the gear you already have

    A phone or a laptop is enough to begin. Test the recording before spending hours on it: can you hear the voice, read the screen and understand what is happening? Record a short sample, play it back and correct the obvious problems.

    Spend time improving the result before spending money on equipment. A quiet room, a sensible microphone position and a tighter edit can be more useful than a new camera.

    3. Edit for the person watching

    Give the opening a reason to keep watching. Remove avoidable waiting, balance the sound and use captions where they help someone follow the video. An effect is useful when it strengthens a moment, not simply because the editor has a button for it.

    My own work mixes gameplay with cartoon clips, composites, music and short jokes. Learning those edits is part of why I keep making videos. For somebody else, a clean explanation with a few well-chosen cuts may be the better approach.

    4. Package the upload clearly

    1. Title: tell someone what they will see. Include a relevant game, subject or project name.
    2. Thumbnail: make one clear focal point readable on a small screen.
    3. Description: explain the video in the first lines, then add useful links and credits.
    4. Chapters: use accurate times and labels when a longer video has distinct sections.
    5. Playlist: place an episode where the next viewer can follow the series.
    A useful check before publishing

    Watch the uploaded version yourself. Check the thumbnail at phone size, listen for abrupt volume changes, test the links and make sure the title describes the actual video. A descriptive title is more useful than a pile of unrelated hashtags.

    5. Learn from the next upload

    Studio can show how people found a video and where they stopped watching. Look for a question you can act on: did the opening take too long, was the subject unclear, or did the thumbnail promise something the video did not deliver?

    A small sample is still a small sample. One quiet upload does not prove the idea is worthless, and one unusually successful clip does not make every future video predictable. Change something purposeful, then keep creating.

    My first attempts: an iPod taped to a chair

    Like a lot of kids growing up alongside YouTube, I wanted to try it. From the outside it looked simple: record a game, upload it and wait for people to find you. I did not understand how much went into the sound, editing or even making the video easy to follow.

    Some of my earliest attempts used an iPod taped to the back of a chair as a camera. There was little commentary, barely any editing and not much thought behind the titles. I had enthusiasm, but I did not yet have the patience or knowledge to turn it into something consistent.

    I also tried a capture device with a tangle of cables designed for older televisions. Having another piece of equipment did not automatically solve the problems. I still needed to understand the process and enjoy doing the work when hardly anybody was watching.

    Coming back to it as an adult

    This time I approached it as a creative project I could develop. I already wanted to play Fallout: New Vegas, so I began recording a series, making individual thumbnails and learning how to put episodes together around the equipment I had.

    Free and accessible tools helped, but the bigger difference was continuing after the first few uploads. I had more patience for fixing a bad recording, trying a different edit and learning a skill that would help the next video. Making the intro or compositing a joke into the game became enjoyable work in its own right.

    The following has grown gradually. People are beginning to recognise and appreciate what I make, and seeing someone choose to come back means more to me than a number in isolation. I am still learning, still using limited equipment and still deciding how I want the channel to develop.

    I do not think there is an age at which you have missed your chance to make things. Start with the story, game or idea you actually want to share. Use what you can access, let the work improve and give other people a chance to find it.

    About monetisation

    Eligibility, geography and policy requirements can change. Subscriber totals alone do not guarantee acceptance or income. Use the current Earn tab in YouTube Studio and YouTube’s official guidance to check what applies to your channel.
