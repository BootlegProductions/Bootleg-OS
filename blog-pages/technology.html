Why Personal Websites Still Matter

A personal website gives your work a home that you can shape yourself, even when the rest of the internet keeps changing around it.

In this postHow nearly ten years of learning web design led me to create Bootleg OS—a personal website, portfolio, archive and testing ground for whatever I decide to build next.

Growing up around technology

I have been building websites in one form or another for close to ten years, but my interest in computers began long before I wrote my first line of HTML.

A lot of that influence came from my father. He was always interested in computers and was a particularly committed Apple Mac fanboy. He wanted to work for Apple but never managed to get the opportunity. Instead, for several years, he ran a small but surprisingly popular mobile-phone repair “shop” from our living room.

People would arrive at the house with smashed screens, failing batteries and iPhones that appeared to have completely given up on life. He specialised in replacing screens and batteries, restoring broken phones and handling the various technical problems people brought through the door.

Our living room regularly contained small tools, spare components, half-open devices and people waiting to find out whether their phone could be rescued. It was not a conventional repair shop, but people knew about it, recommended it to one another and kept coming back.

He had the same sort of sincere love for technology expressed by Kip in Napoleon Dynamite: “I love technology. Always and forever.”

That enthusiasm naturally passed on to me.

We built my first computer together using spare parts that were lying around and whatever components we could salvage. It was not a perfectly coordinated gaming computer assembled from a list of expensive new parts. It was a machine constructed through experimentation, reuse and discovering which pieces could be persuaded to work together.

Building that computer helped remove some of the mystery surrounding technology. A computer stopped being one sealed object that either worked or did not. It became a collection of components that could be opened, examined, replaced and rebuilt.

That way of thinking has stayed with me. Many of my current projects still begin with whatever equipment, software or knowledge I already have available, followed by the question: what can I turn this into?

My first experience with coding

My coding journey properly began at high school, when my teachers encouraged me to pursue computer science.

The earliest lessons covered subjects such as binary and converting values into hexadecimal. I understand why those fundamentals matter, but at the time I found them fairly tedious. Converting rows of numbers did not immediately give me the feeling that I was creating anything.

My interest grew once the work became more practical.

We began learning how to install and operate Ubuntu, use the command-line interface and build basic applications. Working with Linux made computers feel open again. Instead of being limited to clicking the buttons somebody else had designed, I could enter commands, change files and begin to understand what the system was doing underneath its graphical interface.

We then moved into HTML and CSS and started constructing very simple websites.

Those first pages were nothing impressive. They were built from basic headings, paragraphs, links, images and blocks of colour. However, unlike converting a number into hexadecimal, changing a piece of code produced an immediate visible result.

I could alter a line, refresh the browser and see the page respond.

That connection between writing something and watching it become an interface was what made web design feel exciting to me. A blank file could become a page, and that page could be changed into practically anything with enough time and understanding.

Learning to specialise in web design

After high school, I continued into college and began specialising more seriously in web design.

The course involved much more than writing HTML and CSS. It incorporated graphic design, animation, databases, video production, post-production, website design and development. Those subjects initially appeared to be separate disciplines, but over time I began to understand how naturally they could work together.

A website is not only its code.

It also needs a visual identity, readable typography, images, navigation, content and some understanding of the person expected to use it. A database can determine what information appears. Animation can communicate an interaction or give an interface personality. Video can become part of the page rather than something hosted elsewhere. Graphic design can make the difference between a technically functional website and one that somebody actually wants to explore.

That mixture of technical and creative work is what kept me interested.

I have never wanted to work exclusively on invisible systems while leaving somebody else to decide how the finished project should look. I enjoy being able to develop the structure, design the interface, create the graphics and then continue altering all of it until the different pieces feel connected.

University and my final-year project

I later studied Computer Science with Web Design at the University of Huddersfield, graduating with a Bachelor of Science with Honours and achieving a 2:1.

University expanded my understanding of web development, user experience, application design and the processes involved in taking an idea from an early concept to a presentable final project.

For my final-year project, I developed a wrist-mounted application and physical prototype inspired partly by the Pip-Boy from Fallout. The concept explored how a wearable device could monitor personal information, health and biometric data while also functioning as portable storage.

The project required research, interface design, development, testing and a physical presentation. I also created a poster explaining the concept and development process.

That poster received the award for best poster design after the final-year work was displayed and voted on by students and teaching staff. It was a particularly satisfying result because it recognised the combination of visual communication and technical work that had gradually become central to the way I approach projects.

The final-year concept later became the foundation of my Raspberry Pi Pi-Boy project. Rather than allowing it to remain a university submission stored away in a box, I returned to it, rebuilt the application and began developing it into a working Fallout-inspired device.

That is also one of the ideas behind my personal website: older work does not have to remain frozen at the point when a course or assignment ends.

It can be revisited, documented and turned into something new.

Learning outside education

School, college and university gave me a foundation, but most of the development in my skills has happened through working independently in my own time.

Courses have deadlines and assessment criteria. Once an assignment meets those requirements, there is usually a point where it has to be submitted and left alone. Personal projects do not necessarily have that endpoint.

I can continue altering them because I notice something that bothers me, discover a better method or suddenly have an idea months later. I can break them, restore them and rebuild entire sections without worrying about whether the work still matches a marking scheme.

That freedom has allowed me to explore more areas of design and development than I would have encountered through assignments alone.

I have continued building websites, applications, graphics, editing tools, media players and Raspberry Pi interfaces. Most began because I wanted something for my own use and could not find an existing version that worked exactly as I imagined.

That is how Bootleg Broadcast developed from a basic video page into a ten-channel scheduled web television. It is how my Internet Archive scraper grew from a small link extractor into a tool capable of processing much larger collections. It is how a university prototype eventually became a touchscreen Pi-Boy application with maps, inventory management, quests, notes and a working radio.

More recently, my portfolio has expanded to include browser-based creative tools such as an image background remover and pixel-art editor. Each new project forces me to learn something that I did not fully understand when I began it.

Some projects work immediately. Most absolutely do not.

There are broken layouts, failed libraries, functions that solve one problem while producing three new ones and features that appear flawless on one screen before collapsing on another. Working through those problems is where much of the actual learning happens.

I continue building because it keeps my skills active. If I stop using a programming language or piece of software for long enough, parts of that knowledge inevitably begin to disappear. Personal projects give me a reason to practise while also producing something that belongs to me.

A place with its own shape

Social media is useful for finding an audience, sharing updates and placing work in front of people who might never deliberately visit a portfolio.

However, every social platform has already decided how that work should be presented.

Images are cropped into predetermined shapes. Videos are pushed towards particular lengths. Posts move rapidly down a feed and eventually become difficult to find. The design of your profile is largely identical to everybody else’s, while algorithms decide which pieces remain visible and which disappear almost immediately.

A personal website can be organised around the work instead.

For me, that means one site can contain a playable radio, a weather application, interactive tools, a blog, a gallery, university work, graphic design, case studies and ongoing development projects. They do not have to compete for attention inside the same endless vertical feed.

A complex project can have its own page with room to explain where the idea came from, how it changed and what went wrong along the way. Images can be placed beside the relevant part of the story instead of uploaded as an unexplained gallery. A working application can be embedded directly into the site rather than represented by one screenshot and a caption.

The website can have an atmosphere and identity of its own.

Designing beyond the feed

Building my own site means deciding how people move through it.

A visitor might arrive to use one of the applications and then discover the project that inspired it. Somebody looking through the gallery might follow an image into a full case study. A blog post can connect to an interactive tool, while an older university project can sit beside the newer version it eventually became.

Navigation, writing and visual design become part of that experience rather than decoration placed around it.

That freedom creates more work. Pages need to remain readable, navigation needs to make sense and older material requires enough context to be useful. A design that looks distinctive on a desktop monitor still needs to work on a phone. New sections need to feel connected to the existing site rather than pasted on as an afterthought.

The upside is that every one of those decisions can be revisited.

If part of the site no longer works, I can rebuild it. If the visual style becomes too cramped or inconsistent, I can redesign it. If an old project deserves more explanation, I can return years later and write about what I learned from it.

I am not permanently trapped inside the layout decisions of a social-media company.

Bootleg OS: a website and an operating system for my work

Bootleg OS is my attempt to give years of web development, graphic design, experiments and unfinished ideas a proper home.

The “OS” part does not mean that it is literally intended to replace Windows or Linux. It represents the site acting as a connected interface for the different things I create: projects, writing, tools, images, applications and media.

Some pages document finished work. Others contain projects that are still being developed. Several pieces began at college or university and have since been rebuilt using skills I learned later. The applications section gives me somewhere to release small tools that may have started as solutions to my own problems.

The site itself has gone through several visual and structural changes. It has grown from a straightforward portfolio into something more interactive, with a vaporwave-inspired interface, a project archive, a gallery, working applications and articles that explain the thinking behind the work.

Rather than hiding every unfinished experiment until it becomes perfect, Bootleg OS allows the process to remain visible.

That matters to me because the route towards a finished piece is often more informative than the finished piece alone. An early version shows what I understood at the time. A later version shows what I learned by discovering its problems.

Owning your corner of the internet

A personal website provides a degree of independence that social platforms cannot.

The website still depends on hosting, domain services and the wider infrastructure of the internet, so it is not completely beyond outside control. However, I retain far more influence over its organisation, presentation and files than I would receive from a social-media profile.

I can preserve copies of the source code. I can move the project to another host. I can decide whether a page remains available. I can organise the archive without waiting for a platform to introduce or remove a feature.

This feels increasingly important as websites and services constantly change their layouts, policies and priorities. A platform that appears permanent can decline, close or make years of older material difficult to access.

A personal site gives that work another place to live.

It does not need to replace social media. Social accounts can still help people discover the work, while the website acts as the more permanent destination where everything remains connected.

A living archive rather than a finished object

I do not see Bootleg OS as something that will eventually become completely finished.

There will always be another page to improve, another project to document or another tool I want to attempt. Some parts will eventually look dated. Others will need to be rewritten as my understanding improves.

That is not a failure to finish the website. It is part of what the website is for.

It records nearly ten years of learning, beginning with spare computer parts, Ubuntu commands and extremely basic HTML pages. It contains work influenced by high school, college and university, but also the projects I pursued independently because I wanted to discover whether I could make them function.

A personal website can hold the work you are proud of, the experiments that nearly worked and the older projects that show how far you have travelled.

It can be a portfolio when applying for work, a workshop while developing something new and an archive once that work has moved into the past.

Most importantly, it gives you somewhere to continue.

Bootleg OS is not simply a finished display of what I already know. It is the place where I can keep finding out what else I am capable of building.
