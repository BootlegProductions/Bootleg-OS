Sites for Cinephiles

A few useful places to visit when a film sends you searching for its cast, production history, reviews or the next thing to watch.

In this postIMDb for facts and credits, Letterboxd for personal film tracking and discovery, and Rotten Tomatoes for release information and a quick—but sometimes questionable—snapshot of critical opinion.

When a film sends me searching

I rarely finish watching a film without immediately wanting to look something up.

I might recognise an actor but have no idea where I have seen them before. I might want to know how much the film cost, where it was made, whether a sequel is planned or what other people thought its ending meant. Sometimes I become more interested in a character actor who appeared for five minutes than the person whose face occupies most of the poster.

IMDb, Letterboxd and Rotten Tomatoes all serve slightly different versions of that curiosity. They overlap in places, but I do not think any one of them should be treated as the definitive authority on whether a film is worth watching.

A rating can tell you that people reacted strongly to something. It cannot guarantee that their taste has anything in common with yours.

IMDb: facts, faces and falling down rabbit holes

IMDb is the site I use most often because I find it the most useful for straightforward facts and information.

If I want to identify an actor, check a release date, find a director’s other work or see who wrote an episode, IMDb is usually the first place I look. Its interconnected cast and crew pages make it easy to move from one film to another, following an actor, director, writer, composer or production company through their previous work.

What begins as a quick search for “Where have I seen that bloke before?” can easily become half an hour of opening tabs and discovering that the same person appeared in three unrelated programmes I watched years ago.

IMDb also contains information about budgets, box-office performance, filming locations, technical specifications, production companies, alternative titles and release history. Not every entry is equally complete, particularly for obscure or independent films, but the size of its database makes it an extremely useful starting point. IMDb describes its service as including credits, reviews, quotes, trivia, box-office information, recommendations and watchlists alongside its main catalogue.

Trivia and behind-the-scenes details

The trivia section is one of my favourite parts of IMDb.

It contains all sorts of production stories, hidden details, casting changes, mistakes, improvised moments and strange bits of information about well-loved films and television programmes. Some entries are genuinely fascinating; others are incredibly specific facts that somebody cared enough to submit.

The quality can vary because much of the information is contributed by users, so I would not treat every piece of trivia as unquestionable historical evidence. It is still an enjoyable way to continue exploring a film after the credits have finished.

IMDb also includes sections for goofs, quotes, connections and soundtracks. These can help identify a piece of music, find a reference to another film or confirm that the continuity error you noticed was not simply your imagination.

The unexpectedly entertaining Parents Guide

The Parents Guide is intended to help viewers understand potentially unsuitable content beyond a simple age certificate.

Entries are divided into sex and nudity, violence and gore, profanity, alcohol, drugs and smoking, and frightening or intense scenes.

It can be genuinely informative, particularly when deciding whether a film is appropriate for a specific person. An age rating alone does not explain whether something contains one brief violent moment or two uninterrupted hours of misery.

However, the Parents Guide can also be unintentionally hilarious.

Because the descriptions are written so literally and stripped of all dramatic context, a completely ordinary scene can sound like evidence being read aloud in court. Some contributors catalogue every kiss, insult, glass of alcohol and partially visible body part with astonishing dedication. You can occasionally get more entertainment from the guide than the film itself.

IMDb ratings and reviews

IMDb’s rating system is useful as a very broad indication of popularity, but I do not completely trust it.

The scores can sometimes feel flattened or strangely generalised, particularly when thousands of people reduce a complicated reaction to one number. Highly dedicated fan communities, organised negative voting and people rating something before properly engaging with it can all distort the impression created by an average.

The review section can have the same problem. Thoughtful responses sit beside enormous dumps of one-sentence reactions, arguments between fandoms and people declaring something either a masterpiece or the death of cinema.

That does not make the rating meaningless. It simply means I would rather read several reviews and look for recurring points than allow the number at the top of the page to make the decision for me.

IMDb on mobile

IMDb has official applications for both Android and iOS, as well as a mobile version of the website.

The app includes the main title and name pages, trailers, interviews, watchlists and the large cast-and-crew database.

It is particularly useful while watching television, when somebody appears on screen and I need to identify them immediately instead of waiting until the episode ends. The danger is spending the next ten minutes looking through their career and missing what is happening in the programme I originally paused to investigate.

Letterboxd: a personal film diary

Letterboxd approaches films from a more personal and social direction.

Where IMDb often feels like a reference database, Letterboxd feels like a diary shared with an enormous community of film fans. It allows users to mark films as watched, record the date they watched them, give star ratings, write reviews, create lists and maintain a watchlist.

A lot of people I know use Letterboxd specifically to keep track of their viewing history. Over time, a profile becomes a personal record of what somebody watched, when they watched it and how they felt at the time.

The official description calls it “your life in film,” which is a fairly accurate summary. Users can keep a diary, rate films on a five-star scale, follow other members, share reviews and compile lists around practically any subject.

Watchlists that grow faster than they shrink

The watchlist is one of Letterboxd’s most practical features.

Whenever somebody recommends a film or I see an interesting trailer, I can add it to the list instead of trying to remember the title several months later. Once a film is marked as watched, Letterboxd can move it from the watchlist into the user’s watched films and diary.

In theory, this creates an organised list of films to work through.

In practice, my watchlist has a habit of growing considerably faster than I can watch anything on it.

That is not necessarily a bad thing. Browsing the list can be more useful than opening a streaming service and being shown the same handful of heavily promoted releases. It creates a collection shaped by my own curiosity rather than entirely by an algorithm.

Reviews, jokes and film discovery

Letterboxd reviews range from lengthy personal essays to one-line jokes written immediately after the credits.

That mixture is part of the site’s personality. It does mean that the most popular comment is not always the most thoughtful analysis, but Letterboxd is less interesting when treated as a formal review archive and more interesting as a record of how different people responded.

Following users with similar—or interestingly different—taste can lead to films I would never have searched for myself. Somebody might create a list of forgotten British horror films, strange animation, films set almost entirely in one location or every movie featuring a suspiciously specific type of staircase.

Those user-made lists are often more useful for discovery than a general “You may also like” section.

Letterboxd feels personalised because it is built around individual viewing histories, lists and relationships between users. IMDb tells me who appeared in a film; Letterboxd shows me what that film meant to the people who watched it.

Letterboxd on mobile

Letterboxd has official mobile apps designed around logging films, updating a diary, managing watchlists and reading reviews.

The mobile format suits the service particularly well. It is easy to log a film immediately after watching it, add a quick rating or write a short review while the reaction is still fresh.

It also makes Letterboxd dangerously convenient when somebody mentions a film in conversation. I can add it to my watchlist within seconds and then leave it sitting there untouched for the next four years.

Rotten Tomatoes: useful information attached to a strange score

Rotten Tomatoes is the one I use least, largely because I do not particularly like or trust the impression created by its scoring system.

The Tomatometer does not represent the average score critics gave a film. It represents the percentage of approved critical reviews classified as positive. A film receiving mildly positive reviews from almost everybody could therefore have a higher Tomatometer score than a divisive film that some critics considered extraordinary and others hated.

That is probably the most important thing to understand about Rotten Tomatoes.

A score of 90% does not mean the average critic rated the film nine out of ten. It means that 90% of the counted reviews fell on the positive side of its fresh-or-rotten division.

Once understood, the number becomes less mysterious, but I still find it too blunt. Films are not tomatoes, and reducing a collection of different arguments to one large percentage removes most of the reasons those critics liked or disliked the work.

The separate audience score—now called the Popcornmeter—adds another reaction, but it does not solve the wider problem of treating popularity as a substitute for explanation.

What I use Rotten Tomatoes for

Despite my problems with its ratings, Rotten Tomatoes can still be useful for practical information.

I mainly use it to check what is currently being released, what is airing and when new films, television seasons or episodes are expected. Its title pages collect release information, critic and audience responses, trailers and details about where something may be available.

It can also provide a quick sense of the conversation surrounding a release. If critics and audiences reacted very differently, that disagreement may be more interesting than either score on its own.

The reviews themselves are more valuable than the percentage. Reading several excerpts can reveal whether people consistently criticised the pacing, praised a performance or disagreed completely about the tone.

Rotten Tomatoes on mobile

Rotten Tomatoes also has an official mobile app containing film and television pages, Tomatometer and Popcornmeter scores, reviews, trailers, interviews, entertainment news and information about where titles are available.

For somebody who already uses Rotten Tomatoes regularly, the app offers a more convenient way to check current releases and reactions. Personally, I am more likely to use it for release tracking than to let its score decide whether I watch something.

Three sites doing different jobs

Although all three services contain film information and ratings, I think their real strengths are different:

  • IMDb is the reference book: cast, crew, budgets, release details, trivia and the answer to “Where have I seen that actor before?”
  • Letterboxd is the personal diary: viewing history, watchlists, reviews, lists and recommendations from other film fans.
  • Rotten Tomatoes is the reaction snapshot: release information and a quick comparison between critical and audience responses, provided you understand what its percentages actually represent.

None of them needs to become the final authority over what you watch.

Some of my favourite films have mediocre ratings. Some widely praised films have left me cold. A film can be technically flawed, critically divided or completely ridiculous and still be exactly the thing I wanted to see.

These websites are useful for extending the experience: tracing an actor’s career, discovering something obscure, recording what I watched or learning how other people reacted.

They can point me towards a film, but the enjoyable part is still watching it and making up my own mind.
